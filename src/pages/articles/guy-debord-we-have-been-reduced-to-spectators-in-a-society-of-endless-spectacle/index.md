<span class="big-letter">I</span>n the modern industrialized world, life “presents itself as an immense accumulation of spectacles. All that once was directly lived has become mere representation.”

So declares the French philosopher Guy Debord in his utterly prescient 1967 work, <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer sponsored" href="https://link.amazon/B057JBBi4">The Society of the Spectacle</a>, which argues that consumer society replaces authentic social life with the passive consumption of images.

More and more of the world comes to us indirectly, through packaged fragments designed to be consumed. Even when these fragments are totally accurate, they supplant active participation, turning lived events into things we primarily encounter as _representations_. 

Thus social life has become mediated, Debord declares: like consumers choosing between commodities, we have become spectators choosing between spectacles.

The relevance of this analysis to today’s internet-driven mass media hardly needs pointing out. The latest celebrity hysteria, the breaking news catastrophe, the geopolitical crisis: all are delivered to our screens via carefully curated parcels designed to engage, enrage, and titillate.

So enticing are these parcels that what’s on our screens can at times feel more urgent and real than reality itself. The world around us devolves into an irrelevant kind of staging area, useful only for the construction of more spectacle.

Such detachment from reality indicates a more general shift in the human experience, Debord argues. While early stages of capitalism switched the emphasis of human life from “being” to “having”, Debord claims later stages of capitalism encourage us to slide even deeper into unreality: from “having” to “appearing”. 

>An earlier stage in the economy’s domination of social life entailed an obvious downgrading of _being_ into _having_ that left its stamp on all human endeavor. The present stage, in which social life is completely taken over by the accumulated products of the economy, entails a generalized shift from _having_ to appearing: all effective “having” must now derive both its immediate prestige and its ultimate _raison d’etre_ from appearances.

Appearance has become a substitute for reality, and visibility is this new world’s currency. Brand building is not just for media or business; it’s in our politics, our popular conceptions of fame and identity, and even in our social and personal lives. What’s the size of your audience? How much clout do you have? 

The logic insisted upon by the spectacle, Debord suggests, is that

>Everything that appears is good; whatever is good will appear.

The greater the visibility, the greater the value. The share of the market, the share of success, is now governed by the share of attention. 

## So what is the purpose of all this spectacle? What does it do to society? What does it do to us?

<span class="big-letter">I</span>mportantly, Debord does not see “spectacle” merely as a synonym for the mass media we produce and consume. Yes, the production of spectacle is often biased and inauthentic. Yes, we can be concerned with projecting the appearance of status and value to others within our own information bubbles. But all these bubbles ultimately float in a single sea, and that sea is capitalism.

Debord’s core thesis is that spectacle, be it constructed or consumed, is the ultimate reinforcer of capitalistic values. As he puts it:

>The spectacle is not a collection of images, but a social relation among people, mediated by images.

Spectacle governs the reality of the worker outside of work hours, and its goal is to keep the worker entranced, inactive, and receptive to the imperatives of perpetual production and consumption.

Through ceaseless spectacle, capitalism conducts an “opium war” that reduces potentially active citizens into passive, pliable spectators: overwhelmed into inertia by the sheer scale of _things that are happening_, alienated from their own desires, and needful of new things to fill the void. As Debord puts it:

>The spectacle’s function in society is the concrete manufacture of alienation.

The spectator’s alienation from themselves, and accompanying submission to the dominant system, proceed as follows:

>the more he contemplates, the less he lives; the more readily he recognizes his own needs in the images of need proposed by the dominant system, the less he understands his own existence and his own desires [...] the individual’s own gestures are no longer his own, but rather those of someone else who represents them to him. The spectator feels at home nowhere, for the spectacle is everywhere.

Commentaries, critiques, and ‘hot takes’ about the world themselves become aspects of spectacle, a ‘marketplace’ where narratives can be bought, sold, and spun to populate, sustain, and ultimately pacify the worker’s nonworking hours.

Even slogans urging us to disconnect, even this article itself, all are messengers of capital. As Debord writes:

>In all its specific manifestations – news or propaganda, advertising or the actual consumption of entertainment – the spectacle epitomizes the prevailing model of social life. It is the omnipresent celebration of a choice _already made_ in the sphere of production, and the consummate result of that choice. In form as in content the spectacle serves as total justification for the conditions and aims of the existing system. It further ensures the permanent presence of that justification, for it governs almost all time spent outside the production process itself.

When we’re not actively working, when we’re not actively producing for the economy, we’re passively consuming the spectacle that sustains it.

Spectacle is the social glue that binds the infrastructure of late-stage capitalism: it’s the means by which consumerism and the broader economy come to infiltrate and eventually totally dominate every sphere of human life.

## Is spectacle really a unified tool of capitalism?

<span class="big-letter">P</span>erhaps Debord’s analysis seems a bit strong. Is everything we witness (this so-called ‘spectacle’) really a unified tool of capitalism? If so, who’s running the show? Within the vast diversity of human civilization, aren’t there radically competing narratives, groups, and powers vying for our attention and support – some of which actually oppose the dominant ideas of the establishment and the values they express?

Debord isn’t saying the spectacle is run by a shadowy cabal of ultra evil capitalists, but that it’s the structural, inevitable, emergent outcome of a world driven by capital accumulation. The spectacle is an expression of commodity society, and reproduces the social relations of capitalism.

And Debord’s critique was not directed solely at Western consumer capitalism, which he dubbed the “diffuse spectacle”; he also analyzed Soviet-style bureaucratic societies as another form of capitalist domination, which he called the “concentrated spectacle.”

His point is that while there are genuine struggles within and between peoples and nations, all these struggles occur within a capitalist socioeconomic superstructure that has _already conquered_ the human world.

War, catastrophe, revolutions: these simply become another form of spectacle, happening elsewhere, for us to consume on our way to work.

Though the spectacle of conflict plays out daily on our devices, Debord points out that we must never forget that the institutions in conflict 

>have been established for the purpose of running the same socioeconomic system.

It might occasionally wear a different color tie, speak a different language, or follow a different religion, but growth for growth’s sake remains the unrivaled imperative driving most of the globe.

When we get so caught up by the spectacle of commodity society, this imperative fades from view to become a kind of background law of nature: we forget that growth for growth’s sake is a _human choice_.

Spectacle thus undermines our capacity to enact genuine political change to our capitalistic system of governance, and prevents us from seeing the world and ourselves as they really are. 

Through spectacle, capital worms its way to the very heart of lived human experience, to the very purpose of every activity, reducing existence to the status of a trade: rather than just _be_, rather than just _live in_ and _experience_ reality, we ask: right now, what is the most valuable thing I can exchange for my time?

## So what should we do? How can we cut through the blanketing spectacle?

<span class="big-letter">F</span>or Debord, the spectacle is a totalizing system: it cannot be properly critiqued from within nor fixed through existing economic or political machinery.

The spectacle ends only when we seize back control of our immediate everyday existence. We must reject the role of passive, pliable spectatorship and actively construct and live in our own realities, guided by our own imperatives rather than those imposed by the dominant economic system.

This doesn’t simply mean refusing to accept narratives pushed by mass media: I can’t just throw away my smartphone and claim I’ve escaped the spectacle. The inauthenticity and indirectness of our social lives run much deeper. 

Debord’s work was part of the broader Situationist movement of the 1950s-1970s, which played an influential role in the 1968 Paris uprisings.

One Situationist tactic, détournement, involved taking existing cultural images and turning them against their original purpose – parodying advertisements and billboards, for example, to expose the desires and assumptions they were selling. 

Situationists also urge us to reject the primacy of commerce in our land and architecture, to implement direct self-management, and refuse to rely on institutions or ‘leaders’ to act on our behalf in the construction and representation of the world.

Ultimately, Debord thus urges, to escape the spectacle, we need a revolution – in the organization of our social lives, work, and politics. 

Now, revolution isn’t everyone’s cup of tea. If here we’re feeling a bit squeamish, well maybe the harms of spectacle are overblown. Maybe spectacle itself is a bit of a vague idea. Maybe we can just get on with our lives.

In fact, yes, do you mind if we just park this for a second? I’m just going to have a quick look at Instagram.

O, sweet embrace..! Behold the titillating videos, the hot takes, the products I desire, the effortlessness of my thumb scrolling, scrolling, scrolling…

Sorry… what were we talking about again? 

## What do you make of Debord’s analysis?
- Does Debord’s diagnosis that we live in a society of spectacle ring true for you?
- Do you think there might be ways to escape it besides revolution?
- Do you agree that the human experience has shifted from ‘being’ to ‘having’ to ‘appearing’?
- Is it inevitable that everything occurring within capitalism will come to reinforce its paradigms of commodification, exchange value, and growth for growth’s sake?
- Or do you find Debord’s critique a little overblown? Are we really all just passive dupes consuming spectacle? 
- Does it actually matter that capital infiltrates most aspects of human life? Is it really the great evil that Debord suggests?
- And is it maybe a bit convenient for Debord’s theory that even critique of the spectacle can be passed off as more spectacle?
